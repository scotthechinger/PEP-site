The anatomy of a case

Watch how it happens.

When a prosecutor breaks the rules meant to keep a case fair – hiding evidence, using false testimony, charging someone without cause – it's called prosecutorial misconduct. It happens at any stage of a case.

Read more

Stage 01 · Charging

A prosecutor brings the case.

The prosecutor reviews the police investigation and decides whether to charge, what to charge, and how serious the charges will be. Misconduct here means charging someone without enough evidence to justify it, or piling on additional or harsher charges than the case supports to pressure someone into a plea deal. Charging decisions are made almost entirely behind closed doors.

Read more

Stage 02 · Disclosure

The law requires prosecutors to turn over evidence that could help the defense.

Misconduct here means hiding, altering, or destroying evidence so the defense can't use it and the jury never hears about it. This is the most common form of prosecutorial misconduct — and the hardest to catch, because what was hidden often stays hidden.

Read more

Stage 03 · Trial

For the small share of cases that go to trial, prosecutors are in the driver's seat.

Misconduct here includes discrimination in jury selection, putting on testimony the prosecutor knows is false, pressuring a witness into a version of events that isn't true, or telling a jury things the evidence doesn't support.

Read more

Stage 04 · Conviction

A conviction can cost someone years – sometimes their whole life.

A conviction can mean prison time — but even without it, the conviction alone can be devastating: a lost job, trouble getting the next one, a lost home, a family pulled apart. By this stage, the damage is already done.

Read more

Stage 05 · Appeal

Occasionally, long after a conviction, a court finds the misconduct and puts it in writing.

Most cases are never reexamined, so most misconduct stays hidden. When a judge does put a finding in writing, it becomes part of the clearest record there is of how pervasive the problem is.

Read more

Stage 06 · After

What happens to the prosecutor?

When misconduct is uncovered — the hidden evidence, the lost years, the judge's findings — here's what typically happens to the prosecutor: Nothing. Fewer than 2% of prosecutors who commit misconduct face any professional consequence, even after a court has found a violation.

Read more

Why?

Absolute immunity.

Prosecutors can't be sued for misconduct they commit while prosecuting a case — even when it's intentional, and even when it destroys a life. The Supreme Court created absolute immunity in 1976 and has turned down every chance to narrow it since.

Read more

The problem is national. The record is being built in Colorado.

PEP has built the most complete record of prosecutorial misconduct in the country in Colorado. What it shows is not unique to the state. It is what every state would find if it looked. Colorado is also where accountability is closest to reach: 83% of Colorado voters — across the political spectrum — support ending absolute immunity for prosecutors. What gets proven in one state becomes the case for every state. That's why this matters no matter where you live.
